# Xoximilco

*A Night on the Canals in Mexico*

December 23, 2025. Mexico.

Miyera told me this would be an experience. She was right.

I met her at 4:00 pm, a tour guide who also studied psychology, probably in her early 50s. We talked for 30 minutes and connected easily. She told me about her brother, an artist who travels alone. He spent three months wandering Colombia, city to city, chasing restaurants and wine and women. Chasing his flame, she said. His artistic inspiration.

She saw something in me that reminded her of him.

When she learned I was here to relax, that I'd already done the ruins and cenotes and checked the boxes, she offered something different. Not a tour. An experience. Xoximilco.

She wants to celebrate her birthday there someday.

2 hours later, I was in a van.

## The Van

The pickup was at 6:20 pm. I'd booked it 90 minutes after hearing about it.

There were 7 of us in the van. I was the only English speaker.

Everyone else spoke Spanish. Fast, fluid, comfortable. They joked. They laughed. And then Jose, the driver, would turn around and try to translate.

It never worked.

The whole van would erupt at something, and Jose would glance at me in the rearview mirror, pause, then deliver a flat English version that landed nowhere. Everyone laughed again, this time at the gap between what was said and what I received.

I laughed too. That's the thing about being the outsider: if you lean into it, it becomes its own kind of belonging.

## Arriving

When we pulled up, the surprise amplified.

Everyone was Latino. Everyone. Families from Puerto Rico. Couples from Colombia. Groups from Chile and Venezuela. All here for the same thing. All speaking Spanish.

If I wasn't brown, I would have stood out completely.

People spoke to me in Spanish first. When I didn't respond, they switched to broken English. I responded in broken Spanish. We met somewhere in the middle.

I realized I'd stumbled into a pocket of Mexico that tourists like me rarely find. This wasn't designed for Americans. This was Latin tourists coming to experience something deeply Mexican, something from their shared cultural roots.

I was an accident in the best possible way.

## What Xoximilco Actually Is

David, our host for the night, explained it to me later.

This place is a replica. The original is Xochimilco, in Mexico City. The real one has ancient canals, a functioning church, a real cemetery. It sits on a lake surrounded by chinampas, the floating gardens the Aztecs built centuries ago.

Families go there for baptisms, quinceañeras, Day of the Dead. It's sacred and celebratory at the same time.

What they built here in Cancun is a recreation of that experience for people who can't make it to Mexico City. Same trajineras (the colorful boats). Same music. Same food. Same games. A way to bring the tradition to the coast.

But knowing it's a replica doesn't diminish it. The songs are real. The recipes are real. The people sharing the boat with you are real.

## The Night Sky

The sky stopped me before anything else.

You forget, living in cities, that stars can look like this. Bright. Insistent. A crescent moon hung over the water like it had been placed there by a set designer who knew exactly what he was doing.

I took pictures, knowing they wouldn't capture it. You take them anyway.

## The Food

The chicken with chocolate was a discovery.

I've forgotten the Spanish name already, but not the taste. Rich, complex, ancient.

The special shrimp in that black sweet sauce, another discovery. And here's the thing: the Latin folks at my table were discovering some of these dishes too.

This wasn't tourist food dressed up for gringos. This was real, and even the Mexicans and Colombians were trying things for the first time.

The desserts were novel in the true sense of the word. Cinnamon, cacao, caramel, goat milk. Combinations that shouldn't work but do, that feel like they've been perfected over centuries.

And then the coffee.

Café de olla, I think they called it.

Strong, hot, black, with cinnamon and something sweet (maybe honey). They told me to add tequila blanco. I did.

I could become an alcoholic on this combination alone.

I had no tooth and the tequila still tasted great. When the drink is right, nothing else matters.

## The Music

The music was pure.

I mean that literally. No tracks. No backing vocals. No production to hide behind. Just voices and instruments and something you either have or you don't.

Their women sing. Not just technically, though they have that. They feel it. You watch their faces and you know this isn't performance. This is transmission.

I videotaped some of it, asked about the songs that marked me. The melodies are still in my head. At the end of the essay, I added a YouTube link to a song that's stuck in my head.

## The Game

David brought out a black box with two electrodes.

He handed me one, put it in my left hand. Handed the other to the woman across from me. Then he told us to hold hands with the people next to us.

We formed a closed circuit.

All of us, strangers, now literally connected.

The box has levels 1 through 10. As the singers hit high notes, David cranked the dial. The electricity moved through all of us. A young girl, maybe 10 years old, let go first. The circuit broke. Everyone laughed.

This is a famous traditional family game, apparently.

Omar, the guy next to me, told me it's also a bar game. They make bets on who can stand the highest number. In some bars, if you reach 10 and hold for a certain time, you drink free.

When we asked David to join us for a drink after, he declined. They breathalyze the staff after every show. No exceptions.

That's how seriously they take this. The experience is the product. They protect it.

## The People

I was the only non-Spanish speaker on the boat.

Everyone else was from Mexico, Chile, Colombia. All doing this for the first time. All discovering their own culture, or each other's, or both.

I made friends. Their names are already fading but their faces aren't. We shared food and electricity and music and tequila. We held hands in a literal circuit while someone turned up the voltage.

That's how you meet people. Not through small talk. Through shared experience. Through something that demands you be present.

The stars were still out when we finished. Bright. Insistent.

Miyera was right. This was an experience.

Some things you can't capture in pictures. You take them anyway.
